**Ruling on the reporter's notes (specifically requested):** Judge William F. Sullivan issued a February 2025 subpoena requiring The New Yorker to produce "handwritten notes, emails, audio recordings, and voice messages" from reporter Eren Orbey's interviews for his profile of Patrick Clancy (Boston Globe, https://www.bostonglobe.com/2025/03/19/metro/lindsay-clancy-patrick-clancy-new-yorker-magazine-first-amendment/). The New Yorker's attorney Jonathan Albano argued the request was "directly contrary to the First Amendment" and that "the notion that the government could seek presumptively privileged, unpublished information from any news outlet that expresses sympathy for a criminal defendant is chilling" (same URL). The original subpoena was ultimately **quashed on June 2, 2025** on jurisdictional grounds, with leave to reissue in New York; as of October 2025 no materials had been produced and no new subpoena had issued (U.S. Press Freedom Tracker, https://pressfreedomtracker.us/all-incidents/new-yorker-writer-subpoenaed-ahead-of-massachusetts-murder-trial/).

**Bifurcation motion (denied):** The defense sought to split the trial into two phases — the first on the acts, the second on criminal responsibility. Prosecutors argued Clancy "had no Constitutional right to a two-part trial that would basically repeat all of the evidence presented in the first part of the trial." Judge Sullivan took it under advisement (Yahoo/New Haven Register, https://www.yahoo.com/news/articles/former-wallingford-resident-accused-killing-234513787.html). A judge subsequently **denied** the request to split the trial into separate phases; the defense then filed a motion offering to formally acknowledge her involvement in the conduct, which prosecutors did not agree to (Fox News, https://www.foxnews.com/us/massachusetts-mom-offers-admit-killing-3-children-prosecutors-push-back-move-could-dodge-prison.print).

**Competency:** At the October 26, 2023 Superior Court arraignment, forensic psychologist **Dr. Karin Towers** reported Clancy continued to experience depression and suicidal ideation and displayed "flat affect with little emotion," and assessed that Clancy "still poses a serious risk of harming herself"; Judge Sullivan ordered her commitment to remain at Tewksbury Hospital (Boston.com, https://www.boston.com/news/crime/2023/10/26/what-we-learned-from-duxbury-mom-lindsay-clancys-superior-court-arraignment/). A judge also allowed a motion for Clancy to undergo psychiatric examination by "experts chosen by the Commonwealth" (Yahoo/Patriot Ledger, https://www.yahoo.com/news/lindsay-clancy-duxbury-mother-accused-124206593.html). **No source located states an express finding that Clancy was competent to stand trial** — omitted rather than inferred.

### OPENING STATEMENTS — Day 1, Monday, July 27, 2026

**Prosecution (Shanan Buckingham):** Described Clancy taking Cora to a doctor's appointment while Patrick Clancy watched the boys, and searching for takeout, children's MiraLax, and the route between the home, a restaurant and CVS. Buckingham argued Clancy "took the children to the basement, strangled them each with exercise bands" before injuring herself and jumping from a window, and characterized her as someone who "was controlling and wanted to kill the children because she was no longer satisfied with her life." (Court TV, https://www.courttv.com/news/lindsay-clancy-trial-opening-statements-set-for-mom-accused-of-killing-her-3-children/)

**Defense (Kevin Reddington):** "Lindsay Clancy is aware that she killed her children and is not trying to deny that," but "the defendant never wanted to get rid of her children." He argued she suffered from postpartum issues, was incorrectly treated with SSRIs when she needed different care, and was "over-prescribed medications." (Court TV, same URL) Reddington told jurors she "was suffering from postpartum psychosis, a rare mental illness linked to the stress, sleep deprivation and hormonal changes that follow childbirth" (NBC Boston, https://www.nbcboston.com/news/local/lindsay-clancy-trial-opening-statements-live-updates/3987462/). He also said she was "prescribed 13 different psychiatric medications" (WBUR, https://www.wbur.org/news/2026/07/27/lindsay-clancy-trial-opening-statements).

**First witness:** Patrick Clancy, the children's father and Lindsay Clancy's then-husband, testified first (NBC Boston, same URL).

## 4. THE LEGAL STANDARD

### The McHoul test

Massachusetts adopted the American Law Institute Model Penal Code § 4.01 formulation in **Commonwealth v. McHoul, 352 Mass. 544 (1967)**:

> "A person is not responsible for criminal conduct if at the time of such conduct as a result of mental disease or defect he lacks substantial capacity either to appreciate the criminality [wrongfulness] of his conduct or to conform his conduct to the requirements of law."

(Justia, https://law.justia.com/cases/massachusetts/supreme-court/volumes/352/352mass544.html). The court framed this as a restatement of the existing Massachusetts rule in clearer language rather than a substantively new test (same URL).

The two prongs are disjunctive — either a lack of substantial capacity to appreciate wrongfulness, **or** a lack of substantial capacity to conform conduct to the law, suffices. This is reflected in the defense expert testimony in this trial: Dr. Zeizel testified Clancy "was unable to conform her behavior to the rule of law" (PBS, https://www.pbs.org/newshour/nation/lindsay-clancys-defense-rests-at-trial-over-whether-postpartum-psychosis-drove-her-to-killings) and that she could not determine right from wrong on January 24, 2023 (ABC7, https://abc7chicago.com/post/lindsay-clancy-trial-day-16-defense-expert-suggests-clancys-health-care-providers-missed-warning-signs/19702385/).

### Burden of proof

The burden is on the **Commonwealth**, at the **beyond a reasonable doubt** standard. Per **Commonwealth v. Kostka, 370 Mass. 516, 526 (1976)**, quoted in Commonwealth v. Kappler, 416 Mass. 574 (1993): "When a defendant claims that he is not criminally responsible for his acts, the Commonwealth bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant is sane." (Justia, https://law.justia.com/cases/massachusetts/supreme-court/volumes/416/416mass574.html)

The Massachusetts model jury instruction states "the Commonwealth must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was criminally responsible at the time of the alleged offense" (Massachusetts Trial Court Model Jury Instruction 9.200, Lack of Criminal Responsibility, https://www.mass.gov/doc/9200-lack-of-criminal-responsibility/download).

### The presumption (inference) of sanity

**Commonwealth v. Lawson, 475 Mass. 806 (2016)** held that "the inference that a defendant is criminally responsible because the great majority of persons are criminally responsible is not sufficient alone to warrant a rational finder of fact to conclude beyond a reasonable doubt that a defendant is criminally responsible." The court clarified that the "presumption of sanity" is properly an inference rather than a true legal presumption, and that this inference alone cannot satisfy the reasonable-doubt standard where evidence of mental illness is presented. The Commonwealth may prove criminal responsibility through "the inferences arising from the circumstances of the offense" and "admissible evidence of the defendant's words and conduct before, during, and after the offense, including evidence of malingering," and need not present expert testimony in every case. (Justia, https://law.justia.com/cases/massachusetts/supreme-court/volumes/475/475mass806.html)

Model Instruction 9.200 likewise notes that "the inference of sanity always raises an issue of fact for the jury" (https://www.mass.gov/doc/9200-lack-of-criminal-responsibility/download).

### Verdict options in this trial

NewsNation identifies four possible outcomes (https://www.newsnationnow.com/crime/lindsay-clancy-possible-verdicts/amp/):
1. **Guilty of first-degree murder** — "mandatory sentence of life in prison without the possibility of parole"
2. **Guilty of second-degree murder** — "life sentence but with eventual parole eligibility"
3. **Not guilty by reason of mental illness** (lack of criminal responsibility)
4. **Hung jury / mistrial** — permitting retrial

The judge has additionally been asked to put **involuntary manslaughter** on the verdict slip. Judge Sullivan discussed first-degree murder, second-degree murder and involuntary manslaughter for the verdict slip; the defense seeks involuntary manslaughter as an option and the prosecution opposes (Boston 25, https://www.boston25news.com/news/local/lindsay-clancy-trial-live-updates-defense-expected-call-final-witness-before-resting/3SY5KO3XLBG5NJ5F3S2VCFJKUY/). Reddington argued "You can't try this case in a vacuum"; Sprague argued "the evidence is that there was specific intent to cause the death" (Boston Globe, https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/08/20/metro/lindsay-clancy-trial-live-updates/) and that "involuntary manslaughter does not apply because there was a specific intent to kill" (Fox News, https://www.foxnews.com/live-news/lindsay-clancy-murder-trial-day-16).

### What happens on an NGRI verdict — M.G.L. c. 123 § 16

Per the statute (https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartI/TitleXVII/Chapter123/Section16):
- **§ 16(a):** The court may order a person found not guilty by reason of mental illness to be hospitalized at a facility "for a period of forty days" for observation and examination, with provision for Bridgewater State Hospital for males posing a risk of serious harm.
- **§ 16(b):** During that observation period or within sixty days, "the district attorney, the superintendent of a facility or the medical director…may petition the court…for the commitment of the person." A commitment order is valid for six months.
- **§ 16(c):** After the initial commitment expires, commitment may be renewed for "additional one year periods."
- **§ 16(d):** The district attorney "shall be notified of any hearing…and shall have the right to be heard at such hearings."
- **§ 16(e):** Court-ordered restrictions on movement within or from the facility "shall not be removed except with the approval of the court," with notice to the district attorney.

Model Instruction 9.200 describes the same sequence: hospitalization for observation and examination; petition within that period or within sixty days of the verdict for commitment to a mental health facility or Bridgewater State Hospital "initially for a period of six months"; renewable annually, such that a defendant who continues to be mentally ill and dangerous "may remain committed for the duration of his [or her] life," while a defendant who no longer meets those criteria "will be discharged" (https://www.mass.gov/doc/9200-lack-of-criminal-responsibility/download).

NewsNation summarizes the practical effect: after an acquittal by reason of mental illness "the court can order her to be hospitalized for up to 40 days for observation," after which officials may petition for involuntary commitment for an initial six months, "renewable in one-year increments" if a judge finds mental illness and a likelihood of serious harm (https://www.newsnationnow.com/crime/lindsay-clancy-possible-verdicts/amp/).

## 7. MASSACHUSETTS DOCKET ACCESS — HONEST FINDINGS

**The public docket system exists and covers this court.** Mass.gov identifies **www.masscourts.org** as the Massachusetts Trial Court Electronic Case Access portal, covering "District, Boston Municipal, Probate and Family, Superior, Housing, and Land Courts" (https://www.mass.gov/info-details/how-to-search-court-dockets). Superior Court criminal matters — "Criminal complaints," "Indictments," "Probation transfers" — are among the case types available online (https://mass.gov/info-details/types-of-cases-available-to-the-public-online).

**Could I reach it? No.** Every attempt against masscourts.org failed at the network layer from this environment:
- `https://www.masscourts.org/` → robots.txt fetch failed
- `https://www.masscourts.org/eservices/home.page` → robots.txt fetch failed
- `https://www.masscourts.org/eservices/home.page.2` → "robots.txt fetch failed: All connection attempts failed"
- `https://www.masscourts.org/eservices/searchresults.page` → same

Raw `curl` is separately blocked by org egress policy for all hosts, so I could not distinguish between a host-side block, a proxy block, and the site refusing the request. **I did not verify whether masscourts.org is JS-gated or captcha-protected — I could not load it at all.** I will not guess.

**Structural obstacles that are documented, independent of my access failure:**
1. **Name search will not work for this case.** Mass.gov states plainly that "most criminal cases are only available by docket number. If you're searching by docket number, make sure you enter the number exactly how it appears" (https://mass.gov/info-details/types-of-cases-available-to-the-public-online). Party-name searching is limited to civil cases (https://www.mass.gov/info-details/how-to-search-court-dockets). So a docket number is a hard prerequisite.
2. **The docket number is not published anywhere I could find.** It appears in none of: the Plymouth County DA's indictment release, the DA's arraignment release, the DA's district-court arraignment PDF, the Boston Globe, Boston 25, WBUR, NBC News, or the mass.gov documentation. The actual indictment PDF hosted by WBUR (https://media.wbur.org/wp/2023/09/clancy-indictment.pdf) is a **scanned image with no machine-readable text layer** and could not be parsed; it would require OCR.
3. **Remote access is governed by** the Uniform Rules on Public Access to Court Records, Rule 5 (https://www.mass.gov/info-details/how-to-search-court-dockets), and mass.gov directs anyone seeking "the official public record" to "visit the Clerk, Register, or Recorder's office."
4. The system's own search UI requires selecting Court Department, Division and Location before searching, and case-type searches are capped at 30-day ranges (https://www.mass.gov/info-details/how-to-search-court-dockets) — a shape that is unfriendly to scripted polling even where reachable.
